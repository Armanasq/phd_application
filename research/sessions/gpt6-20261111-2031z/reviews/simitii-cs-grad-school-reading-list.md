## What it is
This is a manually curated CS graduate-admissions reading list, not an admissions database, application tracker, or empirical dataset. Its entire value is the selection of external material: official guidance, committee-member perspectives, applicant experience, advisor-visit advice, ranking tools, and sample statements.

The list is predominantly US and computer-science oriented. It does not provide structured coverage of funding, visa rules, country-specific eligibility, or current application cycles.

## Source investigation
All 19 URLs in the README were inventoried and attempted. The most useful source classes are:

1. **Admissions-committee perspective.** Dave Andersen's 2015 reflection describes how one CS department's committee evaluates applications and why research potential, evidence, and fit matter. It is a single institution's account, not a universal admissions policy.
   - https://da-data.blogspot.com/2015/03/reflecting-on-cs-graduate-admissions.html

2. **Applicant experience.** Nelson Liu's 2019 NLP PhD applicant survey aggregates a small, self-selected set of successful applicants. It is useful for identifying recurring practices and uncertainty, but it cannot establish causal success factors or general acceptance probabilities.
   - https://blog.nelsonliu.me/2019/10/24/student-perspectives-on-applying-to-nlp-phd-programs/

3. **Advisor evaluation.** The CMU ML blog's 2020 visit-day guide is useful after an interview or offer, when evaluating advising quality, research culture, funding, and student outcomes. It is not a pre-application eligibility checklist.
   - https://blog.ml.cmu.edu/2020/03/02/questions-to-ask-a-prospective-ph-d-advisor-on-visit-day-with-thorough-and-forthright-explanations/

4. **Statement-writing guidance.** The list includes older guides and sample statements from 2011–2020. These are useful for argument structure and evidence presentation, but examples should not be treated as templates or proof of admission.

5. **Research discovery.** CSRankings is a publication-based CS research index. It can help identify active research groups and compare publication activity in selected areas; it is not a ranking of PhD supervision quality, funding availability, admission probability, or applicant fit.
   - https://csrankings.org/

Other linked material includes a graduate-school application guide by Matt Might, a graduate-school talk by Mor Harchol-Balter, a critique of weak CS PhD statements, and official or university-hosted application guidance. Several links are old or could not be retrieved reliably in this inspection; their current availability and applicability need periodic revalidation.

## What can be learned
- A strong application is built around demonstrated research capability, not a broad list of interests or coursework.
- The statement should connect prior research decisions, methods, results, and the next research question.
- Program fit should be demonstrated through a plausible research connection, not faculty name-dropping.
- Recommendation letters are contextual evidence; their value depends on the recommender's ability to compare the applicant's research contribution with relevant peers.
- Advisor conversations and visit-day questions are a separate stage of the process and should be tracked independently from pre-application outreach.
- Committee-member accounts are valuable but institution-specific. Applicant anecdotes are useful as hypotheses, not as verified rules.
